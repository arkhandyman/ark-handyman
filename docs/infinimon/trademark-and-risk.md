## 1. Is the name available?

A search of USPTO records turned up **no exact match for "Infinimon."** Nearby marks exist (INFINIMO, INFINEON, INFINIGODS, INFINIPLANET) but none in the creature-collector or video-game space.

**What this does not tell you.** A knockout search only catches identical marks. A real clearance search checks:

- Phonetically and visually similar marks ("Infinamon", "Infinemon", "Infinimon" in stylized form)
- Marks in *related* classes, not just identical goods
- **Common-law rights** — an unregistered game already using the name has priority in its territory
- Foreign registers, if you ever ship outside the US
- App-store name collisions, which are enforced by Apple and Google independently of trademark law

That last one bites indies more often than trademark law does.

**Practical next step:** search the App Store, Google Play, Steam, and itch.io for "Infinimon" before anything else. It costs an afternoon.

## 2. Nintendo risk — three different things people conflate

### A. Patent risk — the Palworld situation

**What actually happened.** Nintendo and The Pokémon Company sued Pocketpair (Palworld) in the **Tokyo District Court in September 2024**, over three Japanese patents covering:

1. Capturing a creature by **throwing a device at it**
2. **Aiming** that thrown device
3. **Riding** creatures

**Status as of mid-2026: still ongoing.** A court opinion is expected around **9 November 2026**. Nintendo is seeking an injunction plus roughly **5 million yen (~$30,000)** — which IP analyst Florian Mueller characterized as "chump change for either party, and just a rounding error compared to Nintendo's litigation expenses."

Nintendo's related patents have been taking damage:
- The **US Patent Office rejected** its "summon character to fight" patent in a non-final decision (April 2026)
- The **Japan Patent Office refused** divisional application 2026-019762 in April 2026, reportedly in unusually blunt language, finding the mechanics too close to prior art
- Pocketpair has patched disputed mechanics out of Palworld to reduce exposure

> ⚠️ **A note on sources.** Several low-quality SEO sites claim the case settled in September 2025. Reputable gaming and legal press through mid-2026 describe it as **active litigation**. Treat the settlement claim as unverified.

### Why this matters much less for Infinimon

The asserted patents are about **throwing a capture device** and **riding creatures**. Our taming design — weaken in battle, offer a matching Resonance Crystal, build a Trust meter — involves **no thrown object and no riding**. That was a deliberate call in the very first design conversation, and it holds up well against what Nintendo actually asserted.

Also worth weighing:

- Those are **Japanese** patents. Patents are jurisdiction-specific.
- Pocketpair sold **millions** of copies. Nintendo's economics for pursuing a small indie are terrible — they'd spend far more than they could recover.
- **Game mechanics are not copyrightable** in the US. Nintendo has to reach for patents precisely because copyright doesn't cover "collecting creatures and battling them."

### B. Trademark risk — the name itself

This is the **more realistic** of the two risks, and it's cheaper for Nintendo to act on.

**What Nintendo does enforce:** the **"Poké" prefix**, aggressively. There are roughly 60 "Poké"-prefixed applications on the US register, and Nintendo filed opposition against "Poké Go" arguing likelihood of confusion and dilution.

**What they apparently don't enforce:** the bare **"-mon" suffix**. The strongest evidence is that **Digimon** (Bandai) has coexisted with Pokémon since 1997. "-mon" is a contraction of "monster" — descriptive, and weak as a trademark element on its own. I found no precedent of Nintendo opposing a generic "-mon" suffix.

**But the suffix isn't judged alone.** Likelihood of confusion looks at the *overall commercial impression*: name, goods, trade dress, marketing. "Infinimon" as a creature-collecting game with a Pokémon-like presentation is a meaningfully different risk profile than "Infinimon" as, say, a fintech product. The name is probably fine; the name *plus* imitative presentation is where trouble lives.

### C. Copyright and trade dress

Mechanics are free. These are not:

- Creature **designs and silhouettes** — keep yours clearly distinct
- Creature **names** — no near-misses on existing ones
- **Specific art, UI layouts, sound, music**
- Distinctive **trade dress** — the red-and-white ball, the specific battle-screen layout

## 3. Practical risk rating

| Scenario | Likelihood | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Nintendo patent suit | **Very low** | Wrong mechanics, wrong jurisdiction, terrible economics against a small indie |
| Trademark opposition during registration | **Low–moderate** | Cheap for them; this is the one to actually plan for |
| App-store takedown notice | **Low–moderate** | Cheapest and fastest lever anyone has; not Nintendo-specific |
| Copyright claim | **Low** | Entirely within your control — original art and names |

**Things that would raise your risk sharply, all avoidable:**

- A thrown capture device (a ball you aim and throw)
- Creature riding as a core mechanic
- Any "Poké" prefix, or a slogan echoing "Gotta catch 'em all"
- Creature silhouettes or names close to existing ones
- Marketing that positions the game *as* a Pokémon substitute

The current design avoids every one of these. The taming-through-trust mechanic isn't just thematically better than a capture ball — it's the single biggest thing keeping you out of the patent conversation.

## 4. What registration costs

### USPTO fees (changed 18 January 2025 — TEAS Plus/Standard were eliminated)

| Item | Fee |
|---|---|
| **Base application** | **$350 per class** |
| Surcharge — custom goods/services description not in the ID Manual | +$200 per class |
| Surcharge — insufficient information | +$100 per class |
| Surcharge — each additional 1,000 characters of description | +$200 |
| Statement of Use (intent-to-use path) | $150 per class |
| Extension request (up to 5, intent-to-use) | $125 per class each |
| **Section 8** declaration, years 5–6 | $325 per class (+$100 in grace period) |
| **Sections 8 + 9** renewal, year 10 and every 10 after | $650 per class (+$200 in grace period) |

**Using pre-approved descriptions from the USPTO ID Manual keeps you at the $350 base.** Writing your own adds $200 per class. This is the easiest $200 to save.

### Attorney fees (optional but recommended)

| Service | Typical range |
|---|---|
| Comprehensive clearance search | $300 – $1,000 |
| Filing, flat fee | $500 – $2,500 (average ~$1,120) |
| Office action response, if one issues | $300 – $1,500 |

### Which classes a game needs

| Class | Covers | Needed? |
|---|---|---|
| **9** | Downloadable game software | **Yes** — the core one |
| **41** | Entertainment services, online/non-downloadable games | **Yes** if it's web-playable or you want broader coverage |
| 28 | Toys, games, playthings | Only if physical merch |
| 25 | Clothing | Only if apparel |

### Realistic budgets

| Approach | Cost |
|---|---|
| **Lean** — Class 9 only, DIY, ID Manual descriptions | **$350 – 550** |
| **Sensible** — Classes 9 + 41, attorney, clearance search | **$2,000 – 3,500** |
| **If an office action issues** | add $300 – 1,500 |
| **Ongoing** | $325/class at years 5–6, then $650/class every 10 years |

### Timeline

- **~4.4 months** to first examining action (2026 USPTO average)
- **10–14 months** filing to registration, straightforward application
- **12–18 months** if an office action issues
- Add 3–6 months per office action response cycle

## 5. Recommended plan

1. **Free, this week.** Search App Store, Google Play, Steam, and itch.io for "Infinimon." Also check domain availability. Collisions here are more likely than trademark problems and stop you faster.
2. **Free.** Search the USPTO's own system directly for "Infinimon" and phonetic neighbours. My search was a web search, not a database query.
3. **Pick a backup name you'd genuinely be happy with.** This costs nothing and is worth more than any other item on this list. Renaming in pre-production is an afternoon; renaming after a store listing, art, and an audience is brutal.
4. **File intent-to-use (Class 9) once the name is settled** — before launch, not after. Intent-to-use reserves priority from your filing date while you finish building. ~$350 + $150 Statement of Use later.
5. **Get a clearance search before you spend real money on art.** ~$300–1,000 is cheap insurance against discovering a conflict after commissioning 500 sprites.
6. **Keep the design decisions that are already protecting you** — no thrown capture device, no creature riding, original creature designs and names.
